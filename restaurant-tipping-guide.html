Restaurant Tipping Guide: Percentages, Rules & When to Tip More

Restaurant tipping has one default and a handful of exceptions. Learn the 20% standard, the pre-tax trick that saves you money on every check, and exactly what to do about large parties, bad service, and takeout.

Quick answer Tip 20% of the pre-tax bill for good sit-down service. Drop to 15% for slow or inattentive service, raise to 25% for exceptional service. Large parties (usually 6+) often include an automatic 18–20% gratuity — check before adding more.

The 20% standard, explained

In the US, servers typically earn a reduced hourly wage with tips expected to make up the rest — which is why 20% became the norm for good service. It is not a law and not a moral absolute; it is the going rate, the same way $5 is the going rate for a latte. According to TipCalculatorPro's dining data, 20% is also the percentage most diners actually land on once they do the math.

Quick math: move the decimal one place left on the subtotal and double it. A $64.00 subtotal → $6.40 → $12.80 tip. Round to $13 and move on.

Pre-tax vs post-tax: the detail that matters

Always tip on the pre-tax subtotal. Restaurants print the total prominently, so most people tip on the bigger number without thinking. The difference:

Bill20% pre-tax20% post-tax (8%)You save
$50.00$10.00$10.80$0.80
$85.00$17.00$18.36$1.36
$140.00$28.00$30.24$2.24

No server expects post-tax tipping. The pre-tax number is the professional standard.

Large parties and auto-gratuity

Parties of 6 or 8+ (the threshold is printed on the menu) usually get an automatic gratuity of 18–20%. This is not a suggestion — it is added to your bill. Read the check before adding a tip on top. Double-tipping a large party is the most common tipping mistake in restaurants. If the auto-gratuity is 18% and service was excellent, adding 2–5% extra is a classy move, not an obligation.

When to tip 25% or more

Go above 20% when the server clearly went beyond the job description: handling a complicated allergy order gracefully, comping a dish without being asked, or managing a table of twelve with patience. Our tip more vs less guide walks through more of these judgment calls.

When 15% is acceptable

Tip 15% when service was genuinely off — long unexplained waits, forgotten items, an attitude problem — provided the kitchen was not the problem. Do not punish the server for a slow kitchen or a bad dish; that is management's fault. And if you are ordering at the bar while waiting for a table, see our bartender guide for that etiquette.

Takeout and delivery from restaurants

Takeout pickup: 10% is generous; the kitchen did the work but nobody served you. For food ordered through an app, the driver is a separate worker with separate economics — see our food delivery guide. Ordering pizza specifically? A pizza delivery tip calculator handles the per-pie math, including the "$5 minimum on small orders" rule.

Counter trap: many restaurant takeout counters now show tip screens starting at 20%. You are allowed to choose "custom" and enter 10% — or $0 if you truly just grabbed a bag off a shelf.

Frequently asked questions

Do I tip on alcohol the same as food?

Yes — 20% on the full pre-tax bill including drinks. If you ordered an expensive bottle of wine that required zero extra effort, tipping 15% on the wine portion is an accepted compromise.

What if the service was terrible?

10% sends the message without stiffing someone who may depend on tips. Below 10%, speak to a manager instead — the problem is systemic, not personal. More nuance in when to tip more vs less.

Do I tip the host or the busser separately?

No. Your server tips out the bussers, runners, and bartenders from the tip pool. One tip on the check covers the whole team.

Should I tip more at a cheap diner?

Consider it. 20% on a $9 breakfast is $1.80 — rounding to $3–4 costs you little and means a lot to someone working a breakfast shift. Percentage rules bend at very small bills.
